of Vision-Language Models for Power-Line Inspection}
\author{Anonymous authors}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Anomaly detectors for visual inspection flag where an image looks unusual and leave open what is wrong. Large vision-language models (LVLMs) are expected to say whether a defect is present, what it is and where it lies. We test whether these answers are correct and whether the answers for one image agree with each other; existing power-line benchmarks score each question separately. \emph{InsPLAD-QA} is an MMAD-style benchmark on the public InsPLAD UAV dataset with a manually reviewed, frozen gold set of 246 images and 534 questions. Four open LVLMs and one API pipeline share one prompt, output schema and evaluator. Given the image alone, the presence and type answers of the open models contradict each other on roughly a quarter of the images (consistency error .244--.301). A training-free verification agent raises the macro-F1 of the four-way type question from .522 to .640, and 23 of its 27 additional correct answers are on normal images. Its staged region answer has fewer false alarms and fewer misses than the model's own binary answer, and more misses than a direct region question. An equal-call re-ask does not reproduce these gains. Where position is scored, the reported cell is right less often than a constant ``center'' answer. Missing insulator caps are the main failure: the binary question flags 3 of 27, and no training-free change recovered them on development data. In a follow-up, LoRA fine-tuning on 660 labelled images answers presence and type both correctly on 196 of 226 gold images that share capture dates, and no drone photo, with the training images. The untrained model reaches 98 once its format errors are forgiven, and a small classifier on the same labels 191 (type question alone: 203 against 191). The fine-tuned model names 10 of 23 missing caps and locates defects no better than the constant answer (60 against 62 of 119). We release the benchmark, code and per-question answers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Utilities inspect thousands of kilometres of transmission line with unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and the resulting images are reviewed largely by hand. Unsupervised anomaly detectors can screen such images, but they return only a score and an anomaly map. The map shows where an image looks unusual. It does not say what the defect is, and an inspector must still interpret it. LVLMs promise to answer open questions about such images without task-specific training, and so to explain an alert in words: whether a defect is present, what it is and where it lies. Such an explanation helps only if it is correct and its parts agree. A model that calls an image normal and then names a defect type gives the inspector nothing to act on.

Industrial anomaly-detection benchmarks for LVLMs, notably MMAD~\cite{jiang2025mmad}, show that general models still lag behind human inspectors on manufactured objects. Power-line benchmarks for LVLMs have also begun to appear~\cite{wang2024powerllava,zhong2026powergpt,zhang2026elecvqa}, but as far as we can tell they score each question on its own. Power-line assets differ from bench-top industrial objects: they are photographed outdoors from varying viewpoints, against cluttered backgrounds, and their defects range from surface rust to entirely missing components.

This paper asks three questions. (i) Under a fair, shared contract, how well do current open LVLMs explain defects on power-line assets, that is, say whether a defect is present (Q1), what it is (Q2) and where it is (Q3), and do these answers agree? We use \emph{explanation} in this narrow sense of answers to structured questions and do not score free-text rationales. (ii) How do a normal or defect reference image, anomaly-detector (``vision expert'') cues that show where a detector sees an anomaly, and a training-free verification agent change the answers? The agent re-examines each question separately. For the defect type it adds option definitions and the question's own earlier answer, and for the defect region it asks a presence question before a position question. (iii) Why do missing components remain undetected, and what evidence would detect them?

Our contributions are answers to these questions and one follow-up experiment that grows out of the third:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A benchmark.} InsPLAD-QA is a reproducible benchmark built from InsPLAD~\cite{vieira2023insplad}. It has two tracks, an exact-hash \emph{Aligned Core} of 1,378 images shared by both releases, and a manually reviewed, frozen gold set (246 images, 534 questions on defect presence, type and location), with leakage controls on reference selection and the development split. Four open LVLMs and an API pipeline are compared image-only under identical prompts, schema and scoring, and we report consistency between questions and a constant baseline for every question alongside accuracy (Sections~\ref{sec:benchmark} and~\ref{sec:baselines}).
  \item \textbf{An analysis of references and re-verification.} The four open models are tested with normal, defect and combined references and with 20 vision-expert configurations. A question-independent verification agent, selected on a disjoint development set and run once on gold, is compared with a matched-call re-ask that has the same number of calls but no definitions or staging. Most of the agent's additional correct type answers are on normal images; its staged region-presence answer has fewer false alarms and fewer misses than the model's own binary answer but does not locate defects better, and the re-ask does not reproduce the gain (Sections~\ref{sec:baselines} and~\ref{sec:agent}).
  \item \textbf{A failure analysis of missing caps.} No question detects them reliably. On development data, training-free interventions did not recover them and InternVL3.5-8B did not report them even on a crop of the annotated location, while a supervised classifier did. We relate this to the defect being an absence among repeated parts, supported by prior findings on logical anomalies and LVLM perception (Section~\ref{sec:missing}).
  \item \textbf{A follow-up with a few hundred labelled images.} Dropping the training-free premise, we fine-tune one LVLM on a few hundred labelled images, fix a scoring protocol and score gold once. Defect presence and type rise substantially but not clearly above a small classifier trained on the same labels, and missing caps and defect position remain unsolved (Section~\ref{sec:sft}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Power-line inspection.} Utilities increasingly inspect transmission lines with UAV images analysed by deep learning~\cite{nguyen2018review}. Public data mostly serve trained, task-specific models. Tao et al.\ detect insulators and their defects in aerial images with cascaded CNNs and release CPLID~\cite{tao2020cplid}. STN PLAD annotates five asset types for multi-size detection in high-resolution UAV images~\cite{vieira2021stnplad}, and InsPLAD adds anomaly-detection and fault-classification splits over 17 asset types~\cite{vieira2023insplad}. These datasets were built to train detectors and classifiers rather than to question an LVLM.

\paragraph{LVLM benchmarks for power-line inspection.} Question-answering data for power-line inspection have appeared more recently. Power-LLaVA fine-tunes an LVLM on inspection dialogues and evaluates it on PowerQA, 1,000 multiple-choice questions generated with GPT-4V and refined by hand, covering equipment function, nearby hazards and surroundings~\cite{wang2024powerllava}. PowerGPT, a multimodal foundation model for power inspection, is evaluated on its own benchmark, PowerBENCH, which includes grounding and referring-expression tasks~\cite{zhong2026powergpt}. Closest to our work, ElecVQA-Bench derives about 57,000 closed-set binary-screening and seven-way defect-typing items from InsPLAD crops, re-partitions them into new splits, and compares zero-shot and LoRA-adapted LVLMs with vision-only classifiers; the LVLMs' apparent advantages there depend on input resolution and data partition~\cite{zhang2026elecvqa}. As far as we can tell, these benchmarks score each question separately and do not report whether a model's answers to related questions agree or how reference images change them. InsPLAD-QA keeps InsPLAD's official splits and a manually reviewed gold set, and measures agreement between questions, the effect of references and re-verification, and which defects remain undetected.

\paragraph{LVLMs for industrial anomaly detection.} AnomalyGPT~\cite{gu2024anomalygpt} fine-tunes an LVLM with simulated anomalies, while MMAD~\cite{jiang2025mmad} benchmarks general LVLMs with multiple-choice questions on industrial objects. MMAD also studies agent-style aids such as expert anomaly maps and retrieved domain knowledge. We adopt MMAD's question format and its normal-reference (one-shot) setting, apply them to power-line UAV imagery, and add explicit cross-question consistency metrics.

\paragraph{Anomaly detectors as experts.} We give the LVLMs cues from four unsupervised detectors and chose one from each of four families, so that a cue effect is not tied to one design. PatchCore~\cite{roth2022patchcore} keeps pretrained patch features of normal images in a memory bank and scores a patch by its nearest-neighbour distance. ReConPatch~\cite{hyun2024reconpatch} first learns a contrastive projection of those patch features. Dinomaly~\cite{guo2025dinomaly} reconstructs DINO features and trains one model for many object types. AnomalyCLIP~\cite{zhou2024anomalyclip} compares image patches with learned object-agnostic normal and abnormal text prompts and needs no training on the target domain. All four output an image score and an anomaly map, and none uses defect-type labels, so their output shows where an image looks unusual but not what the defect is.

\paragraph{Self-verification.} Iterative self-feedback can improve LLM outputs~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}, but asking a model to reconsider without new information can also degrade correct answers~\cite{huang2024cannotselfcorrect}. This motivates our matched re-ask control: it spends the same number of calls as the agent but supplies no new evidence.

\subsection{Source data and label policy}
InsPLAD~\cite{vieira2023insplad} provides two releases. The unsupervised anomaly-detection release covers 17 asset categories and 27,620 JPEG images split into train and test (6,481 test images). Three of its assets (\texttt{damper-preformed}, \texttt{spacer}, \texttt{yoke}) are normal-only in test. The supervised fault-classification release covers 5 assets and 11,525 images in train and val (6,417 val images); it has no test split. Folders named \texttt{good} or \texttt{normal} map to \emph{normal}, and every defect folder maps to \emph{anomalous}. We only slug-normalize \texttt{corros\~ao} to \texttt{corrosao} and never merge it with \texttt{rust}. The integer IDs in the supervised annotation file are asset-local class indices, not binary labels, so semantic labels come from folder names.

\subsection{Tracks and Aligned Core}
Track~A (general anomaly detection) uses all 6,481 unsupervised test images in zero-shot and one-shot (normal reference) form. Track~B (structured fault understanding) uses the 6,417 supervised val images and asks for both the anomaly label and the defect class. The two releases share 8,990 SHA-256-identical images; 1,384 of them lie in both unsupervised test and supervised val. Six of these carry conflicting binary labels and are excluded, which leaves an \emph{Aligned Core} of 1,378 images. On these images zero-shot, one-shot and structured answers can be compared byte for byte.

\subsection{Gold v2 QA set}
Running every model on every configuration over the full tracks is costly, and several labels needed manual checking. We therefore built a gold set from the Aligned Core: all 139 of its defective images, plus normal images capped per asset at the median and chosen for diversity with DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} embeddings. Every image was manually reviewed and none was excluded. The set was then frozen (Table~\ref{tab:gold}). Each image has a binary question (Q1: ``Is the target power-line asset normal or anomalous?'') and a four-way defect-type question (Q2: rust/corrosion, bird nest, missing cap, normal). Forty-two images also carry an MMAD-style four-option localization question (Q3), for 534 questions in total; the later Q3 variants are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:q3sets}. Option order is fixed per question and independent of the answer. Figure~\ref{fig:qa} shows one gold image per class with its ground truth and the answers of the direct question and the verification agent (Section~\ref{sec:agent}).

\subsection{Development set and protocol}
Every method choice was made on a separate development set. It has 240 images (480 Q1/Q2 questions; 125 normal, 115 defective) drawn with seed 42 from the supervised \emph{train} split, because the Aligned Core's defective images are all in gold. Dev excludes any image whose size and CRC32 fingerprint occurs in unsupervised test, in a fixed reference, or in gold. Configurations were frozen in a committed file before their single gold run, and gold scores never fed back into configuration choices. Gold had been used for earlier baseline comparisons, so we do not call it an untouched holdout.

\subsection{References and leakage controls}
A one-shot reference is a normal image of the same asset from unsupervised \emph{train}. We exclude any candidate whose fingerprint appears in test. The \emph{fixed} reference is drawn once per asset with seed 42. The \emph{retrieved} reference is the DINOv2-base cosine nearest neighbour of the target (pinned weights). Retrieval exposed a shortcut on dev: normal targets often retrieved a reference from the same capture date (44\% vs.\ 16\% for defective targets), and cosine similarity alone separated normal from defective images with AUROC .886. These numbers are recomputed from the committed dev image list. We therefore exclude same-capture-date candidates (\emph{diffdate}) in every retrieved condition used by the agent.

\subsection{Metrics}
For Q1 we report accuracy and F1; balanced accuracy (BAcc), precision, recall and TP/FP/TN/FN are in the released result files. For Q2 we report accuracy and macro-F1 over the classes present, and the agent tables also report Q2 BAcc (mean recall over the classes present). Q2 has four options including ``normal'', so these scores also reward correct ``normal'' answers on normal images; the agent tables therefore add the number of defective images typed correctly. Invalid outputs count as wrong. For Q1, an invalid answer on a defective image is a false negative. On a normal image it is kept separate from false positives, so it lowers specificity but not precision. \emph{Joint accuracy} counts images where Q1 and Q2 are both correct. \emph{Consistency error} is the share of images with valid answers where Q1 and Q2 disagree (normal with a defect type, or anomalous with ``normal''). \emph{False defect rate} is the share of normal images given a defect type. Differences between policies use a paired bootstrap over images with 2,000 resamples~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}.

Q3 is evaluated in four ways, on different image sets (Table~\ref{tab:q3sets}). The original four-option question covers only 42 images and is used for the image-only baselines. The nine-region question of the agent run (Section~\ref{sec:agent}) is asked for all 246 images and scored for presence, and its saved answers are also scored for position on the 133 defective images with a location box (113 drawn by hand, 20 from InsPLAD-seg instance labels). A separate multi-region run asks for every defect cell. Each score is compared with a constant answer.

\section{Models and Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}
\subsection{Setup}
We keep one model per model line: Qwen3.5-4B~\cite{qwen35} with thinking disabled, InternVL3.5-8B~\cite{wang2025internvl35} through its official chat path, MiniCPM-V-4.5~\cite{yao2024minicpmv} and LLaVA-OneVision-Qwen2-7B~\cite{li2024llavaov}. Three of the four use Qwen-series language backbones, so they are not fully independent. We also include a two-stage API pipeline (Luxia document vision followed by a 32B language model). All open models run unquantized with greedy decoding, using Transformers 5.14.0 as pinned in the Colab notebooks, on a GPU with at least 20\,GB of memory. The notebooks allow an L4 or an A100; the agent, matched-call and MiniCPM re-runs record an NVIDIA L4 (22\,GB).

MiniCPM-V-4.5's chat interface samples by default (temperature 0.7), which we noticed only after its first gold runs. We therefore re-ran its image-only and three reference conditions greedily with unchanged prompts, images and references (2,136 calls, 0 errors, 0 invalid outputs). Its rows in Tables~\ref{tab:baseline} and~\ref{tab:conditions} are these greedy runs. Only its 20 vision-expert configurations remain single sampled runs (marked $^\dagger$). Greedy decoding changed 54 of 534 image-only answers. Sampling had inflated its image-only inconsistency: the consistency error drops from .341 to .280 and the false defect rate from .411 to .308 under greedy decoding. In the reference conditions the table metrics move by at most .02 (Q3 accuracy by .05; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:minicpm}). All agent experiments are greedy.

InternVL uses a fixed total budget of six visual tiles per sample, divided among the images (each multi-tile image also gets a thumbnail). Adding one reference halves the target's tiles, and adding two leaves it a third, so a reference adds context at the cost of target resolution.

\subsection{Image-only baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:baseline} shows that image-only Q1 F1 ranges from .555 to .718 and Q2 macro-F1 from .360 to .505. The original four-option Q3 is barely informative. Its answers are only ``no region'' (22) or ``center'' (20), so a constant ``no region'' answer already scores .524. The best models (.667) beat it by just 6 of 42 questions. We therefore treat Q3 as auxiliary and later replace it with a nine-region question scored for presence and, where a location box exists, for position (Table~\ref{tab:q3sets}).

\subsection{References and vision experts}
Table~\ref{tab:conditions} adds reference images and expert cues for the four open models. The first is the fixed normal reference. The second is a \emph{defect} reference, the DINOv2-nearest anomalous image of the same asset from supervised train, chosen without the target label. The third gives both references together. The fourth is 20 vision-expert cues: four detectors crossed with five presentations. PatchCore (WideResNet-50-2) is fit per asset and Dinomaly as one model for all assets, both on leakage-safe train normals. ReConPatch-lite is our lightweight re-implementation of ReConPatch on the same backbone, and AnomalyCLIP runs its official pretrained checkpoint zero-shot. Each detector's output is shown as a within-asset percentile score, a region-of-interest crop, a heatmap overlay, or the score together with the crop or the heatmap.

A normal reference raises Q1 F1 for all four open models, for example InternVL from .597 to .794. Balanced accuracy, which weighs normal and defective images equally, rises clearly only for InternVL (.663 $\rightarrow$ .751) and Qwen (.579 $\rightarrow$ .625); LLaVA barely moves (.600 $\rightarrow$ .616). For MiniCPM, F1 overstates the gain: it now flags 86 of 107 normal images instead of 22 (recall .518 $\rightarrow$ .978, specificity .794 $\rightarrow$ .196), so its Q1 balanced accuracy falls from .656 to .587. Its effect on coherence is mixed. Consistency error falls for InternVL (.301$\rightarrow$.215) and MiniCPM (.280$\rightarrow$.110). It rises for Qwen (.264$\rightarrow$.341) and more than doubles for LLaVA (.244$\rightarrow$.593). For InternVL and MiniCPM the fewer contradictions come with many more normal images labelled with a defect type: the false defect rate rises from .346 to .617 and from .308 to .841 (MiniCPM $+.533$, paired CI $[+.430,+.634]$). A higher Q1 score therefore need not mean better fault understanding.

With a defect reference, three of the four models detect almost no defect (Q1 F1 $\le$ .014) and call nearly every target normal; only Qwen keeps some detection (.596). The reference is the DINOv2-small nearest anomalous image of the same asset among 2,670 supervised-train defects, retrieved without the target label (mean cosine .67--.90 per asset). Most assets have one defect type in train, so the reference usually shows the target's own defect (Figure~\ref{fig:defectref}), and 14 references are even cropped from the same photograph as their target. The simple explanation, that a model answers ``normal'' because the defect shown is absent from the target, therefore fits poorly. MiniCPM-V-4.5's per-question answers show the opposite (Table~\ref{tab:defectref}): it called 105 of the 106 targets whose reference shows their own defect type normal, including all 27 missing caps. Adding the normal reference restores detection (63 of 106). We read this as a role confusion: a single reference image is taken as the normal standard, as in the usual one-shot setting, so a target that resembles it is judged normal despite the prompt's label. Per-question answers for this condition are available only for MiniCPM-V-4.5, and we did not test the role hypothesis directly (for example by relabelling the same reference), so it remains a hypothesis.

Vision-expert cues trade one question against the other. Averaged over the 20 configurations, they lower Q1 F1 for InternVL (.597$\rightarrow$.382) and MiniCPM (.583$\rightarrow$.417, comparing its sampled runs) and raise Q2 macro-F1 by at most .04. Q1 balanced accuracy falls for three of the four models (InternVL .663$\rightarrow$.551) and stays at .60 for LLaVA. Per configuration, 12 of the 20 raise Q2 for all four models (by up to $+.093$). Q1 F1, however, rises in only 3 of 20 configurations for each of Qwen, InternVL and MiniCPM, and no configuration raises both Q1 and Q2 for every model. Expert scores are percentiles within the 246-image cohort (transductive), which favours the expert condition.

\section{A Question-Independent Verification Agent}\label{sec:agent}
\subsection{Design}
The agent starts from the direct answer with a retrieved (diffdate) normal reference. This \emph{direct} condition differs from the fixed normal reference of Table~\ref{tab:conditions}; with it, InternVL's Q1 F1 is .776 and its balanced accuracy .777. Each question is re-examined on its own: a re-check sees only the reference and target images, the question and that question's own first answer. No other question's answer is shown, used for routing or overwritten. Contradictions between questions are computed only after all final answers are stored (Figure~\ref{fig:agent}). Q1 is never re-checked, because a dev pilot that re-asked every question collapsed InternVL's Q1 F1 from .82 to .08, so Q1 is identical across policies.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Q2, verification agent.} A ``second look'' prompt shows the model's own first answer, which may be right or wrong, together with one-sentence visual definitions of the four options.
  \item \textbf{Q2, revised candidate.} A fresh question with revised definitions of all four options (adding rust cues) and no prior answer.
  \item \textbf{Q3.} A separate nine-region-plus-``none'' question is asked for every image. A presence question comes first, and only a positive answer triggers a full-frame $3\times3$ position question. We score Q3 mainly for \emph{region presence} (a region versus ``none''), so a wrong region on a defective image still counts as ``defect present''. Position is scored separately on the 133 defective images that have a location box (113 drawn by hand and 20 from InsPLAD-seg instance labels; six rust images had no locatable defect). A \emph{position hit} means that the reported cell contains the center of one of the image's boxes, the cell rule of our multi-region run (Section~\ref{sec:missing}). We added this position score after the gold run and computed it from the saved answers.
\end{itemize}

The agent and candidate were chosen on dev (the candidate post hoc, from the additional 200 dev images) and run once on gold with InternVL3.5-8B. That run used 1,606 calls (492 direct answers and 1,114 tool calls) with zero errors and zero invalid outputs.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:agent} and Table~\ref{tab:ci} summarize the gold run. Against the direct question, the agent improves Q2 macro-F1 by $+.118$ (95\% CI $[+.043,+.199]$) and the candidate by $+.102$ ($[+.030,+.177]$); Q1--Q2 joint accuracy rises by $+.085$ and $+.093$. Q2 is a four-way question that includes ``normal''. Of the agent's 27 additional correct answers, 23 are on normal images (63 $\rightarrow$ 86). On the 139 defective images, correct types rise only from 93 to 97 for the agent and to 94 for the candidate (Table~\ref{tab:perdefect}). Macro-F1 rises more than these counts suggest for two reasons. Fewer images receive a wrong defect label, which raises the precision of the defect classes; the direct question, for example, calls 43 images without a missing cap ``missing cap'', the agent 17. Bird nests are also recognised more often (7 $\rightarrow$ 19 of 23). The candidate is not distinguishable from the verification agent (Q2 macro-F1 $-.017$, CI $[-.073,+.036]$).

The staged Q3 raises region-presence BAcc by $+.277$ and cuts normal false regions from 93/107 to 16/107. The direct region question, however, reports a region on almost every image: on 93 of the 107 normal images, all 112 rust and bird-nest images and 23 of the 27 missing caps. Its presence BAcc (.551) is therefore barely above that of a constant ``region'' answer (.500). The model's own binary Q1, asked once with the same reference and shared by all policies, is a stricter comparison. Q1 raises 17 false alarms and misses 40 defects; the staged Q3 raises 16 and misses 27, so it is better on both counts. Its large drop in false regions, by contrast, appears to reflect mostly how weak the direct region question is.

Position does not improve. The reported cell is a position hit on 29 of the 133 located defects for the direct question and on 31 for the agent (paired difference $+.015$, CI $[-.045,+.075]$), while answering ``center'' for every image would score 72. Counting only images where a region was reported, the hit rate is 22\% for the direct question and 29\% for the agent. The staging thus removes some wrong cells without placing the remaining ones better.

Relative to the direct question, the agent misses more defects. Defective images judged defect-free rise from 4/139 to 27/139, and the CI of this false-none increase excludes zero ($+.165$, $[+.103,+.234]$). Most of the extra misses are missing caps. The staged Q3 reports a defect region on only 9 of 27 missing-cap images, against 23 for the direct question (bird nests 23$\rightarrow$18 of 23, rust 89$\rightarrow$85 of 89). Because the direct question reports a region almost everywhere, its 23 are weak evidence that the model recognised the caps: Q1 flags only 3 of the 27. Defect typing shows the same weakness (Table~\ref{tab:perdefect}). Normal images and bird nests improve, while rust drops slightly (79$\rightarrow$73 for the agent, 74 for the candidate). Missing caps fall from 7/27 to 5/27 for the agent and to 2/27 for the candidate, which calls 25 of the 27 ``normal''. The additional 200 dev images were used to choose the candidate (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dev}). On them, the increase in Q3 misses replicates (2$\rightarrow$15 of 99) but the defect-type pattern does not: there the agent and candidate raise missing-cap Q2 from 1 to 5 of 21.

\subsection{Structure or more calls? A matched-call control}\label{sec:matched}
Per image, the agent makes two Q2 calls and up to three Q3 calls, where the direct question makes one of each. To test whether the gain is just the extra calls, we add a \emph{matched re-ask} policy. It spends the same Q2 and Q3 calls on every image as the agent but adds neither definitions nor staging. Q2 gets one own-answer re-check without definitions. Q3 gets as many sequential own-answer re-checks as the staged Q3 used for that image. We repeat the shown answer rather than the identical prompt, because the identical prompt under greedy decoding would trivially reproduce the first answer. The agent's saved gold calls are reused, so only the 622 control calls are new. The committed protocol fixed the primary comparison (agent $-$ re-ask, paired bootstrap) before the run. We read a CI that excludes zero as evidence that the gain is not explained by additional calls.

In this run, asking again already made the model somewhat more conservative, and the agent's additions turned that shift into a large net gain while enlarging the miss rate.

\section{Why Missing Components Are Missed}\label{sec:missing}
No question detects missing caps reliably. On gold, Q1 flags 3 of the 27, Q2 names the defect in 7 (direct), 5 (agent) and 2 (candidate), and the staged Q3 reports a region on 9. The direct region question reports one on 23, but it also does so on 87\% of normal images. A separate multi-region run on gold, whose direct question was less biased (29 of 107 normal false alarms), asked for every defect cell with fixed references; it missed 25 of the 27 caps with both direct and staged prompting. All 27 gold cases are glass insulators, an asset with only five gold normals, so asset and defect effects are confounded. We studied missing caps on development data only, never gold. Dev240 contains 25 of them, of which 4 fall in the 40-image design subset used for most model swaps. Each intervention used fixed settings, and where a pass criterion was defined it was written before the run. One exception: the cross-model re-check was re-run once with a changed proposal format after invalid outputs (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:negative}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Input and wording.} Doubling the target's visual tiles, short defect-evidence prompts, and both together all reduced missing-cap recall on dev240 against the revised-definition Q2 control. Recall fell from 6/25 to 2, 4 and 1 of 25 respectively.
  \item \textbf{Other models and detectors.} Swapping in Qwen3-VL-8B or Ovis2.5-9B for Q2 recovered none of the 4 design-subset missing caps (1 $\rightarrow$ 0 for both). As the Q3 presence model, Qwen3-VL cut missing-cap misses on dev240 only from 10 to 8 of 25 while adding other errors. Three anomaly detectors used for Q3 presence (AdaCLIP, AnomalyVFM, InCTRL) all lowered presence BAcc.
  \item \textbf{Part evidence.} Three tools failed their predeclared reliability gates on normal glass insulators (6 of 7 needed): open-vocabulary detection with Grounding DINO (3 of 7), counting with CountGD++ (2 of 7) and SAM masks (4 of 7). SIFT registration passed its synthetic controls but aligned 0 of 8 real reference--target pairs. DIFT correspondence passed 0 of 7 known-flip checks. None could serve as part-count evidence.
  \item \textbf{Crops of the location.} The model was shown crops of three kinds (20 positive images each): the candidate cell, the human-annotated cell and the annotated box. InternVL answered ``no region'' in all 60 calls, but this prompt also showed the model's earlier empty answer. A separate question without that cue asked only whether a component is missing or broken, and the model answered ``absent'' in all 60 calls. Only positive images were tested.
  \item \textbf{A supervised expert.} We trained a frozen DINOv2 feature plus a logistic head on glass-insulator train labels, using date-disjoint internal splits. The dev images may share capture dates with training. The head detected 25 of 25 dev missing caps with 1 false alarm on 25 dev normals; its normal false-alarm rate on the date-disjoint internal test was 13.8\%. Given to the LVLM as a suggestion to re-check, it raised dev missing-cap Q2 from 6 to 14 of 25, against 8 for a matched re-ask. This breaks the training-free premise and covers one asset only. Occlusion analysis of the head placed the exact grid cell in 12 of 24 cases, against 8 of 24 for a fixed center guess (paired CI includes zero). Integrated gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017ig} scored at most 9 of 24. With 32 integration steps, however, 49 of 52 maps failed the completeness check, so this test of IG is inconclusive.
\end{itemize}
In summary, training-free changes to input, wording, model or added tools did not make the 8B LVLM register missing caps, even with the region cropped for it. Explicit evidence from a supervised classifier did change its answers. These results are consistent with a perception limit for this defect type, which prompts and routing still modulate: on gold, the agent's own definitions and staging lowered missing-cap answers.

\paragraph{Why this defect may be hard.} We did not test the following explanations one by one, but they are consistent with our data and with prior work.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It is an absence.} Rust and bird nests add texture or an object that can be seen; a missing cap removes a part, so recognising it requires knowing which part should be there. MVTec LOCO calls a required object that is not present a \emph{logical} anomaly, as opposed to the local \emph{structural} anomalies, such as scratches and dents, that dominate earlier datasets~\cite{bergmann2022loco}.
  \item \textbf{It sits among repeated, overlapping parts.} A glass insulator is a string of similar stacked discs, so a gap has to be found by comparing or counting neighbouring parts. LVLMs miscount shapes that touch or overlap~\cite{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind}, and in our probes the counting, detection and segmentation tools failed their reliability gates even on normal glass insulators. LVLMs also tend to report objects that usually co-occur with what is in the image~\cite{li2023pope}, which would favour seeing a complete string.
  \item \textbf{Size alone does not explain it.} Cropping helps LVLMs perceive small details in general~\cite{zhang2025mllmsknow}, but doubling the target's tiles lowered missing-cap recall here, and crops of the annotated location did not help.
  \item \textbf{One reference does not show what is missing.} Strings differ in length, viewpoint and background, so a single normal reference of the same asset cannot show which disc should carry a cap, and gold has only five normal glass insulators. Even a defect reference that showed a missing cap did not help: with it, MiniCPM-V-4.5 called all 27 missing caps normal (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).
  \item \textbf{Labelled examples help.} Our supervised head found all 25 dev missing caps, and on ElecVQA-Bench, InternVL3.5-8B adapted with LoRA on labelled InsPLAD crops recalled 69.85\% of missing caps on that benchmark's own split~\cite{zhang2026elecvqa}. The protocols differ from ours, but both point to many varied or labelled examples, rather than one reference, as the evidence this defect needs. In Section~\ref{sec:sft}, an LVLM fine-tuned on images that include 60 missing caps named all 25 on dev and 10 of 23 on gold images that share no drone photo with the training images.
\end{itemize}

\section{Follow-up: Fine-Tuning on a Few Hundred Labelled Images}\label{sec:sft}
The previous section points to labelled examples as the evidence this defect needs. We therefore dropped the training-free premise and checked what changes when an LVLM is fine-tuned directly on a few hundred labelled images. We developed the method on dev240, then fixed a scoring protocol and scored gold once. Dev240 comes from the same pool as the training images, so only the gold numbers are reported as results of this section. The prompts and the reference differ from the earlier sections, so these gold numbers are compared only with each other.

\paragraph{Setup of the first run.} We fine-tuned Qwen3.5-4B, one of the baseline models, with LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora} (rank 16, $\alpha$ 32, applied only to the linear layers of the language model, vision encoder frozen, one epoch of 587 steps, no quantization). The training images come from the five assets of supervised train, 60 per asset--class pair: 300 defective and 300 normal. Before sampling we excluded the dev240 images, every crop cut from the same drone photo as a dev240 image, images whose fingerprint appears in unsupervised test (this covers all of gold) and the reference images. Polymer \emph{corrosão} was left out, following the rule that it is not merged with rust.

Input and output follow a tool-choice agent format. Modelled on the zoom and normal-reference tools of AgentIAD~\cite{miao2025agentiad}, the model emits one JSON action per call: request a normal reference, request a zoomed crop of a region it specifies, or give the final answer, with at most three calls per question. The normal reference is the most similar normal image of the same asset by DINOv2 among those taken on a different date. Training trajectories were built from the labels. A defective image is reference $\rightarrow$ crop $\rightarrow$ answer; a normal image is reference $\rightarrow$ answer, or passes through one crop. The crop regions were not annotated by a person; they were taken automatically where the DINOv2 patch features of the target and its reference differ most.

There are four evaluation conditions. \emph{Single call} gives the target and the normal reference and calls once. \emph{Fixed crop} adds a center-60\% crop. \emph{Tool choice} gives the target only and lets the model choose tools. \emph{Matched re-ask} gives the target and the normal reference and asks again, showing the model its own answer, as many times as tool choice used on that question. The same conditions were run on the model before training.

\paragraph{Development on dev240.} We developed the method on dev240. Dev240 comes from the same supervised-train pool and capture dates as the training images and has been explored repeatedly, so its scores are used only to choose the method and are not reported as results. The table and the details are in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:sft}. On dev240 the first model answered Q1 and Q2 both correctly on 233 of 240 images. Tool choice did not raise the scores, however, and 341 of 365 crop requests pointed at the same corner regardless of the image. With the automatic training boxes the model did not learn where to zoom. The first-run training images also contain no polymer-asset defect, while gold has 29.

\paragraph{Second run: crop regions drawn by a person.} Because the first run's crops did not reflect where the defect is, we trained once more and changed only the training data. The model, the LoRA settings, the prompts and the answer format are the same. Three things changed. First, for the 360 defective images the crop region and the correct Q3 cell come from boxes drawn by a person instead of the automatic boxes (the largest box, with each side grown to at least 0.22 of the image side). Second, 60 polymer \emph{corrosão} images were added to the 600 of the first run, giving 660. They are asked and answered as rust in training only; the evaluation labels and gold are unchanged. Third, the trajectories of normal images no longer contain a crop: reference $\rightarrow$ answer. Because the three changed together, a difference from the first run cannot be read as the effect of any one of them. Training took one epoch of 599 steps. On dev240 the second model is at the level of the first (235 of 240 images, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:sft}).

\paragraph{Gold scoring protocol.} Before the run we fixed in a document which models and conditions are scored, the metrics, and the test each claim must pass; gold was then scored once. Q1 and Q2 are the gold questions as they are. Q3 is the nine-cell question used on dev240 (with a ``no region'' option), scored with the position-hit rule of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}. The normal reference follows the dev240 rule: the nearest DINOv2 neighbour among normal images of the same asset in unsupervised train that were taken on a different date. Twenty of the 246 gold images (6 normal, 14 defective) come from the same drone photo as a second-run training image. The primary scope is therefore the remaining 226 (101 normal, 125 defective, 119 of them with a location box). All 226 still share a capture date with a training image. The training images cover all 35 capture dates, so no image would remain if shared dates were removed as well. The primary result is the second model with a single call. The untrained model and the first model were also scored with a single call and with tool choice, and for the second model we added the fixed crop and the matched re-ask as controls. Differences between conditions are judged by the 95\% CI of an image-level paired bootstrap (2,000 resamples).

In the same run we also checked what the same labels give a small dedicated classifier. Using only the 660 second-run images and their labels, we fitted a logistic regression once on frozen DINOv2 CLS features (scikit-learn defaults; no setting was chosen on gold or dev240). The classifier sees the target image only and outputs one of normal, rust, bird nest or missing cap; Q1 is scored as anomalous when the output is not normal. It gives no location.

\paragraph{Gold: defect presence and type.} The effect of fine-tuning carries over to gold, at a lower level than on dev240 (Table~\ref{tab:sftgold}). With a single call, the second model answers Q1 and Q2 both correctly on 196 of 226 images (.867). The untrained model reaches 64 (95\% CI of the difference $[+.518,+.646]$). This 64 includes format errors, however. Of its 678 answers, 356 were rejected because they were not the required JSON, and some of them state the answer plainly, for example ``B. Anomalous.'' After the run we therefore re-scored the untrained model while accepting such outputs (the $\dagger$ row of the table): a single option letter, a letter followed by exactly that option's text, and an explanation that ends with one final-answer JSON object. The rules were fixed from the shapes of the outputs before any re-scored number was computed, and no answer was inferred from the meaning of a sentence. Scored this way the untrained model reaches 98 images (.434), and the difference from the second model remains large (CI $[+.367,+.500]$). Explanations that choose no option stay invalid, which leaves the untrained model without an answer on 19\% of Q1 and 74\% of Q3. The effect of fine-tuning is therefore read from two values on the same 226 images: 98 (.434) for the re-scored untrained model and 196 (.867) for the fine-tuned model. With the shared prompt of Section~\ref{sec:baselines} and a normal reference, the same untrained model had a joint accuracy of .476 on the 246 gold images; the prompt, the reference and the image set differ, so that value is not directly comparable. The same condition scored .979 on dev240, so gold is about 11 points lower. Most of the drop is in missing caps. Q2 names 77 of 83 rust images, 17 of 19 bird nests and 99 of 101 normal images, but only 10 of 23 missing caps. Q1 misses 23 of 125 defective images, 18 of them missing caps. The same model named all 25 missing caps on dev240. It raises a false alarm on 4 of 101 normal images. The first model is correct on 200 images, and the CI of its difference from the second model includes zero ($[-.053,+.013]$). Scoring all 246 images gives a similar picture (211 images, .858, for the second model with a single call).

\paragraph{Comparison with a small classifier.} The classifier trained on the same 660 images answers Q1 and Q2 both correctly on 191 of 226 images. The difference from the second model (196) is five images, and its CI includes zero ($[-.022,+.066]$). On Q2 alone the second model is correct on 203 images and the classifier on 191, and the CI of this difference excludes zero ($[+.009,+.097]$). The classifier found all 25 missing caps on dev240 but only 3 of 23 on gold. With this number of labels we therefore cannot say that fine-tuning an LVLM is clearly better than a small classifier at defect presence and type. What the classifier cannot do is answer where the defect is.

\paragraph{Gold: defect position.} The primary result, the single call, does not locate defects better than a constant answer. The reported cell is a hit on 60 of the 119 located defects, and a constant ``center'' answer is a hit on 62 (CI of the difference $[-.118,+.084]$). By the test fixed before the run, we cannot say that the fine-tuned model locates defects. It located none of the 23 missing caps and answered ``no region'' on 22 of them.

Tool choice differed only in position. It reached 73 hits, more than the single call (60, CI of the difference $[+.025,+.193]$), the matched re-ask (59, $[+.034,+.193]$) and the fixed crop (54, $[+.059,+.261]$), and none of the three CIs includes zero. More calls or a crop of a fixed region did not produce this difference, which suggests that letting the model choose where to zoom helped its position answer. The CI of the difference from the constant answer (62) still includes zero ($[-.017,+.202]$). By defect type the hits are 60 of 77 for rust (constant answer 42), 9 of 19 for bird nests (8) and 4 of 23 for missing caps (12), so only rust is clearly above the constant answer. Q1, Q2 and joint accuracy did not differ between tool choice and the single call (joint accuracy .867 for both; CI of the Q2 macro-F1 difference $[-.074,+.021]$). Tool choice used 2.5 times as many calls (1,675 against 678).

\paragraph{Crop regions.} Unlike the first model, the second model's crops varied with the image. In the primary scope its 319 crop requests covered 245 different regions, and the most frequent region occurred 3 times. For the first model, 356 of 401 requests were the same region, and its tool-choice condition reached only 7 position hits. On Q3 the second model requested a crop on 102 of 125 defective images and on 3 of 101 normal images. Of the 119 located defects it requested a crop on 96, and on 84 of these the crop contained a box center. A fixed center-60\% crop, however, contains a box center on 90 of the 119. We had fixed the test for ``the crop points at the defect'' as containing a box center more often than the fixed region, and this test was not met.

The fixed-crop condition exposed a different problem. Given a crop in advance, the model called 53 of 101 normal images anomalous. In the training trajectories a crop occurs only for defective images, so the model appears to have learned the crop itself as a sign of a defect. This condition named the most missing caps, 16 of 23, but together with these false alarms.

\paragraph{What this does and does not show.} The results support three claims. First, fine-tuning on a few hundred labelled images removes the format errors and raises defect presence and type substantially (from 98 images for the untrained model without format errors to 196), and the effect remains on gold images that share no drone photo with the training images. Second, the number of images with Q1 and Q2 both correct is not clearly different from that of a small classifier trained on the same labels. Third, a model trained with boxes drawn by a person locates defects more often when it chooses where to zoom than when it answers in a single call. Three claims are not supported: that the fine-tuned model locates defects better than a constant answer, that tool use helps defect presence or type, and that labelled examples solve missing caps. After training on 60 examples, missing caps stayed below one half on gold. The limitations are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item One model was trained once per adapter and decoded greedily.
  \item Gold was already used by the methods of the earlier sections, and it comes from the same site as the training images. The primary scope removes shared drone photos only. All 226 images share a capture date with a training image, so these scores are for other drone photos taken on the same days. Generalization to another day or another site is untested, and this dataset cannot test it.
  \item Dev240 comes from the same supervised-train pool and capture dates as the training images and has been explored repeatedly, which is why its scores are in the appendix only. That they are higher than on gold appears to reflect this closeness.
  \item The small classifier is one feature model and one linear classifier. A tuned setting or a fine-tuned vision model could do better, so the comparison bounds the question from one side only.
  \item The second run differs from the first in three ways at once, so the difference between the two models cannot be assigned to one cause.
  \item Differences between models (against the untrained model, the first run and the classifier) were computed after the run from the saved per-image results. The method was fixed before the run. The re-scoring of the untrained model was not planned before the run; the values in the main table are the original scores.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\paragraph{What the agent changes.} In our run, the verification agent made the model more conservative. The matched-call control suggests that part of this conservatism comes simply from asking again~\cite{huang2024cannotselfcorrect}, and that the agent's definitions and staging channel it into a real gain. Compared with the direct region question, the agent removes most false alarms on normal images and misses more defects. That trade-off is partly a property of the comparison: the direct question reports a region on almost every image, so almost any answer that discriminates would remove false alarms and add misses relative to it. Against the model's own binary question the staged answer is better on both counts. Most of the agent's additional correct Q2 answers are on normal images, and it does not locate defects better. Single summary scores hide such distinctions. We suggest that benchmarks report the normal false-alarm rate and per-class recall alongside macro-F1, and compare each answer with constant baselines.

\paragraph{What we propose.} In our data, missing components are the main risk. On gold the staged Q3 answered ``no region'' on 27 defective images, 18 of them missing caps. A missing component can lead to a line failure, while a false alarm costs a second look, so we do not recommend using the agent's ``no defect'' as a final decision. Our data suggest three directions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reviewing ``no defect'' answers.} Because the direct question almost always reports a region, the 102 images (41\%) on which it disagrees with the staged Q3 are nearly the same as the 118 (48\%) on which the agent answers ``no region''. If a human reviews the 102 and is right, misses fall from 27 to 3 (all missing caps); reviewing all 118 removes every miss. Either way 16 false alarms remain. Disagreement therefore adds little over reviewing every ``no defect'' answer, and both rules send close to half of the images to a person. We computed this post hoc on gold to illustrate the idea and did not tune a review rule. A cheaper filter would need a signal that registers missing caps, and none of the LVLM answers we collected provides one.
  \item \textbf{Explicit evidence for absence defects.} Prompts, crops and other LVLMs did not make the model register missing caps, but an asset-specific supervised head detected all 25 dev missing caps, and its suggestion changed the LVLM's answers. Adaptation on labelled examples, as in ElecVQA-Bench, is another source of such evidence (Section~\ref{sec:missing}). A hybrid in which a small supervised detector can veto ``normal'' for high-risk classes would trade the training-free premise for safety. An LVLM fine-tuned on 660 images that include 60 missing caps, and a classifier trained on the same labels, both found every dev missing cap. On gold images that share no drone photo with the training images they found 10 and 3 of the 23 (Section~\ref{sec:sft}). Such a detector has to be validated on images away from its training images.
  \item \textbf{Reporting misses explicitly.} Macro-F1 and joint accuracy rose while missing-cap detection fell. Per-class recall, recall at a fixed normal false-alarm rate, or a cost-weighted score could expose such a shift.
\end{itemize}
Whether larger LVLMs show the same trade-off is open; we tested only 4--8B open models.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (1) The agent was evaluated with one model (InternVL3.5-8B), so transfer to other models is untested. (2) Gold has 246 images and prior exploratory use, and dev was explored repeatedly, so dev gains are exploratory. (3) Missing caps occur in one asset only, which confounds defect and asset. (4) Position is weak and its labels are coarse. On the 133 located defects the agent's reported cell is a hit on 31 (direct question 29), and in the multi-region run the exact cell set matched on 12 of 133 with either prompt. A constant ``center'' answer scores 72 and 46 on these two measures. One cell per box center under-describes large or spread defects. (5) Vision-expert scores are transductive percentiles. (6) No new untouched holdout exists, because the Aligned Core's defective images are all in gold. Unused defective images remain only in supervised train. (7) MiniCPM-V-4.5's 20 vision-expert configurations come from sampled decoding. Its baseline and reference rows were re-run greedily.

\section{Conclusion}
InsPLAD-QA provides a hash-aligned, manually reviewed benchmark for LVLM power-line inspection with a shared prompt, schema and evaluator. On it, normal references raise anomaly-detection F1, but balanced accuracy rises clearly only for InternVL3.5-8B and Qwen3.5-4B and falls for MiniCPM-V-4.5, and their effect on coherence is mixed. Anomaly-detector cues tend to trade anomaly detection for defect typing. A question-independent verification agent improves the four-way type answer, although most of its additional correct answers are on normal images. Its staged region-presence answer has fewer false alarms and fewer misses than the model's own binary answer, while the direct region question it replaces reports a region almost everywhere; it does not improve position. An equal-call re-ask does not reproduce the gain, which points to the agent's definitions and staging. Read as explanations, the answers are partial. The models name some defect types, but with the image alone their presence and type answers contradict each other on roughly a quarter of the images (consistency error .244--.301), and where we scored position they locate defects less often than a constant ``center'' answer. No question detects missing caps reliably, and on development data none of the training-free interventions we tried recovered them, even on crops of the annotated location. Our analysis relates this to the defect being an absence among repeated parts, for which labelled examples, rather than a single reference, appear to supply the missing evidence. In a follow-up, fine-tuning one LVLM on 660 labelled images answered defect presence and type both correctly on 196 of the 226 gold images that share no drone photo with the training images (the untrained model reached 98 even after its format errors were forgiven). These 226 images share capture dates with the training images, so the result does not speak for another day or another site. The fine-tuned model was not clearly better than a small classifier trained on the same labels (191), although on the type question alone it was ahead, 203 against 191. Its position answers stayed at the level of a constant answer. Trained on images that include 60 missing caps, it named 10 of the 23 missing caps in gold. We did not vary the amount of training data, so this result does not show how many examples the defect needs.

\paragraph{First run on dev240.} The fine-tuned model answers almost all of dev240 correctly (Table~\ref{tab:sft}). With a single call, Q1 flags all 115 defective images and raises a false alarm on 3 of 125 normal images. Q2 names all 25 missing caps, 70 of 70 rust images, 17 of 20 bird nests and 122 of 125 normal images. No output is invalid, and Q1 and Q2 are both correct on 233 of 240 images. In Section~\ref{sec:missing}, InternVL3.5-8B named 6 of the same 25 missing caps with the revised-definition Q2, and 14 when given the supervised head's suggestion.

The gap between before and after training cannot be read directly as a gain in perception. Before training, with a single call, the model wrote 419 of its 720 answers as prose instead of JSON, and they were scored as invalid; most of its low score comes from this format error. Under tool choice the untrained model never used a tool either (one call per question).

Tools did not raise the scores. After fine-tuning, tool choice used 2.5 times as many calls as a single call (1,805 against 720), and every metric in the table was equal or slightly lower. Joint accuracy was .967 against .971, with a paired bootstrap 95\% CI for the difference of $[-.013,+.000]$. Q1 and Q2 were both correct on 234 images with the fixed crop and 233 with the matched re-ask, practically the same as the single call (233).

The learned tool use did not reflect where the defect is. The model requested the reference and then a crop on all 115 defective images, and only the reference on 119 of 125 normal images, so the crop request repeated the anomaly decision. Of 365 crop requests, 341 pointed at the same upper-left corner regardless of the image. The automatic training boxes are hard to predict from the image alone, and the model appears to have collapsed to the most frequent one. Dev240 has position labels only for 24 missing caps, so position was not scored.

\paragraph{Overlap with the training images.} No dev240 image comes from the same drone photo as a training image. Sixty dev images have a training image of the same asset and label, taken on the same day, within five frames. Q1 and Q2 were both correct on 58 of these 60 and on 175 of the other 180, so a nearby frame did not raise the score.

On dev240 the second model is at the level of the first (the bottom two rows of Table~\ref{tab:sft}). With a single call, Q1 and Q2 are both correct on 235 of 240 images, and all 25 missing caps are named. Tool choice gives 234, and the paired bootstrap 95\% CI of the difference, $[-.025,+.013]$, includes zero. Dev240 has almost no position labels, so whether the crops point at the defect can be seen only on gold.

\section{Reproducibility}
Code, prompts, frozen configurations, Colab notebooks, leaderboards and per-question answers for the main results are in the project repository (\url{https://github.com/arnold6444/lvlm_insplad}), together with a summary of every follow-up run. The numbers in the tables come from the result files under \texttt{results/latest/}. Some dev follow-ups in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:negative} (the other LVLMs, anomaly detectors, cross-model re-check and part-evidence and alignment probes) ran from frozen source bundles; their code is archived rather than in the repository. Raw per-call logs (prompts, outputs, latencies) and all of this archived code are kept with SHA-256 hashes and available on request. Runs record the model ID, backend, benchmark commit, protocol and status; the later runs (agent, matched-call, MiniCPM re-run) also record the GPU. Notebooks verify the exact code revision before inference and resume from checkpoints saved every 20 calls. For the fine-tuning follow-up (Section~\ref{sec:sft}) we release the scoring protocol fixed before the run, the training record, and the leaderboards and per-image results of every run; the adapter weights and the per-call logs are kept with SHA-256 hashes. InsPLAD images are used under the dataset's CC BY-NC 3.0 license; Figures~\ref{fig:pipeline}--\ref{fig:agent} are generated by \texttt{make\_figures.py} from the released result files.
